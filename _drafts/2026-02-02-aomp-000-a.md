#### Background

The goal of this challenge is not to comprehensively study every concept but to name any concept that you can identify in the featured image and animation, and to understand how they relate to MRI physics. Details treatment of each concept will be covered in future posts in the AoMP series.

The core foundations of MRI physics are the Fourier transform and the interaction of the microscopic magnetic moment (a.k.a., spin) of proton (i.e., hydrogen atom's nucleus) with the external magnetic field. 

The original of the Fourier transform is unarguably the Fourier series, which was first introduced by Joseph Fourier in his 1822 book "Théorie de la chaleur" (The Theory of Heat).

Regarding the microscopic magnetic moment of proton there are several related milestones:
 
+ Isidor Rabi, 1944 physics Nobel laureate, is considered the discoverer of the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) phenomenon. In 1937-1938, Rabi and his research group at the Columbia university successfully measured the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) of lithium nuclei using a modified Stern-Gerlach apparatus, where a beam of lithium chloride (LiCl) was passed through an oscillating magnetic field in vacuum before entering the apparatus. This discovery laid the foundation for the development of NMR spectroscopy and MRI. Using a similar setup, they also measured the magnetic moment of the proton in a beam of hydrogen molecules.

+ Although credited as an inventor of NMR phenomenon, Rabi's experiment setup was inherently limited to basic science research and was not suitable for practical clinical applications. Until 1940s when Felix Bloch and Edward Purcell independently discovered the NMR phenomenon in bulk materials (water and paraffin, respectively), which then paved the way for the development of NMR spectroscopy and MRI. Both Bloch and Purcell were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1952 for their discoveries.

+ Zeeman effect, discovered by Pieter Zeeman in 1896, is the splitting of spectral lines of atoms when they are subjected to a magnetic field. The Zeeman effect provided the first indirect evidence for the existence of electron spin and its interaction with magnetic fields. Zeeman effect is a mysterious riddle that puzzled physicists for decades until the discovery of electron spin and the development of quantum mechanics. The Zeeman effect is a direct consequence of the interaction between the magnetic moment of electrons (which arises from their spin) and the external magnetic field, which leads to the splitting of energy levels and thus the spectral lines.
  
+ Stern and Gerlach's experiment in 1922 provided the first direct evidence of the quantization of angular momentum, which is a fundamental property of particles with spin. The Stern-Gerlach experiment showed that when a beam of silver atoms was passed through a non-uniform magnetic field, the atoms were deflected in discrete directions, proving the existence of electron spin.

+ The concept of spin was first theoretically formalized in the context of quantum mechanics by Wolfgang Pauli in 1927. Pauli introduced three 2-by-2 matrices, which were used to extend the Schrödinger equation to the so-called Pauli equation, to describe the behavior of spin-1/2 particles and the interaction of the particle's spin with an external magnetic field. Though the Pauli equation can describe the Zeeman effect, it cannot explain the fine structure of the hydrogen atom, which is a direct consequence of relativistic effects. Additionally, the Pauli equation is a hand crafted extension of the Schrödinger equation to include spin, and it does not provide a fundamental explanation for the origin of spin. 

+ The Dirac equation, formulated by Paul Dirac in 1928, is often considered the most fundamental description of spin-1/2 particles, as it is an unification of quantum mechanics and Einstein's theory of special relativity. From the Dirac equation, electron's spin emerges naturally as a consequence of the relativistic effects. More than that, the Dirac also predicts the existence of antimatter, which was later confirmed with the discovery of the positron in 1932 by Carl Anderson. Note that, The Pauli equation can be derived from the Dirac equation in the non-relativistic limit.

Bloch's and Purcell's discoveries of NMR phenomenon in bulk materials are often highlighted as the key milestones that paved the way for NMR spectroscopy and MRI. However, for the quest of seeking the ultimately beautiful foundations of MRI physics, I would choose the discovery of the Dirac equation as the most fundamental milestone, because the spin concept that underlies the NMR phenomenon and MRI is beautifully and naturally emerges from the Dirac equation.

$$ \text{Reconstructed image} = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\text{Acquired data}\right\} $$

The sphere and arrow represent a proton with its microscopic magnetic moment that is precessing around the main magnetic field in an MRI scanner.

The true signal is designed in an MRI shape and the red line represents the Fourier series approximation of the true signal with different numbers of Fourier coefficients.

Notably, you see the so called "Gibbs phenomenon" in the Fourier series approximation, which is a fundamental property of Fourier series that arises when approximating a function with jump discontinuities. The Gibbs phenomenon is characterized by an oscillatory behavior with overshoots and undershoots near the discontinuity. In MRI, the Gibbs phenomenon can manifest as ringing artifacts around sharp edges in the image, which is a direct consequence of the Fourier series approximation of the underlying signal.

The fact that deep connection to the Fourier series and Fourier transform arises from the precessing magnetic moment of protons around the main magnetic field, which then induces a time-varying magnetic field that can be detected by the MRI scanner, and this signal is in a purest form can be modeled as sinusoids, which are the basis functions of the Fourier series. The Faraday's law of induction states that a changing magnetic field induces an electromotive force (EMF) in a nearby closed loop of wire, which is how the MRI scanner detects the signal from the precessing protons. The Fourier transform is then used to reconstruct the spatial distribution of the protons (i.e., the image) from the acquired signal in k-space.

#### The Dirac equation

For general audience, the Schrödinger equation is often referred as the fundamental equation of quantum mechanics. 

$$ i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 + V(\mathbf{r}) \right]\psi(\mathbf{r}, t) $$

where $\psi$ is the wave function of the quantum system, $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, $m$ is the mass of the particle, $\nabla^2$ is the Laplacian operator representing the kinetic energy, and $V(\mathbf{r})$ is the potential energy as a function of position.

Despite its fundamental role in quantum mechanics, the Schrödinger equation is unable to explain the spectral line splitting observed in the hydrogen atom, which is a direct consequence of the electron's spin. 

To insert the spin into the wave equation, Pauli proposed the Pauli equation, which describes the behavior of spin-1/2 particles in an external magnetic field. The Pauli equation can be written as: 

$$ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle = \left[\frac{1}{2m}\left(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A\right)^2- q\hbar\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\mathbf B+ q\phi\right]|\psi\rangle$$

where $\|\psi\rangle$ is the two-component spinor wave function, $\hat{\mathbf p}$ is the momentum operator, $q$ is the charge of the particle, $\mathbf A$ is the vector potential, $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ are the Pauli matrices representing the spin operators, $\mathbf B$ is the magnetic field, and $\phi$ is the scalar potential.


> "How did you find the Dirac equation? Professor Dirac?"
> <p style="font-size: 1.0em; font-style: italic; text-align: right;">"I found it beautiful."</p>
> <p style="font-size: 1.0em; font-style: normal; text-align: right;">-- Sir Michael Berry, "Paul Dirac: the purest soul in physics", Feb 01, 1998 </p>

Dirac equation is engraved on the floor of the Abbey of Westminster, London, UK, as part of the memorial to Paul Dirac. (Source: https://www.westminster-abbey.org/abbey-commemorations/commemorations/paul-dirac)

Similar to MRI, you don't need to know any quantum mechanics leave a lone the Dirac equation to understand the basic principles of MRI. However, quantum mechanics and the Dirac equation pave the way to the discoveries and understanding of everyday MRI. 

The same goes to CT, you don't need to know any quantum mechanics or the Dirac equation to understand the basic principles of CT, but quantum mechanics and the Dirac equation pave the way to the discoveries and understanding of everyday CT. 

Whenever I see the Dirac equation, I can't help but to think about its beauty that encapsulates the underlying principles of the most advanced medical imaging modalities: MRI, CT, and PET.

Dirac equation is a foundation for quantum electrodynamics (QED), which is the quantum field theory of electromagnetism. QED describes how light and matter interact, and it is the most accurate theory of electromagnetic interactions to date. 

Schrodinger is unable to describe both the fine structure of the hydrogen atom and the zeeman effect because it does not account for the electron's spin and relativistic effects. The pauli equation can describe the Zeeman effect but not the fine structure of the hydrogen atom, because it does not account for relativistic effects. The Dirac equation can describe both the fine structure of the hydrogen atom and the zeeman effect, because it accounts for both spin and relativistic effects.

Dirac even explain the hyperfine structure of the hydrogen atom, which is a small splitting of the energy levels of the hydrogen atom that arises from the interaction between the electron's spin and the magnetic field produced by the proton's spin. 

Dirac can explain the gold color of gold, which is a consequence of the relativistic effects on the electrons in gold atoms. The Dirac equation predicts that the inner electrons in gold experience a strong relativistic contraction, which leads to a shift in the energy levels and results in the characteristic yellow color of gold.
