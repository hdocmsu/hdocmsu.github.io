## Solution to the AoMP-000-Q challenge

### Background

The goal of this challenge is not to comprehensively study every concept but to name any concept that you can identify in the featured image and animation, and to understand how they relate to MRI physics. Details treatment of each concept will be covered in future posts in the AoMP series.

The core foundations of MRI physics are the Fourier transform and the interaction of the microscopic magnetic moment (a.k.a., spin) of proton (i.e., hydrogen atom's nucleus) with the external magnetic fields. 

The original of the Fourier transform is unarguably the Fourier series, which was first introduced by Joseph Fourier in his 1822 book "Théorie de la chaleur" (The Theory of Heat).

Regarding the microscopic magnetic moment of proton there are several related milestones:
 
+ Isidor Rabi, 1944 physics Nobel laureate, is considered the discoverer of the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) phenomenon. In 1937-1938, Rabi and his research group at the Columbia university successfully measured the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) of lithium nuclei using a modified Stern-Gerlach apparatus, where a beam of lithium chloride (LiCl) was passed through an oscillating magnetic field in vacuum before entering the apparatus. Using a similar setup, they also measured the magnetic moment of the proton in a beam of hydrogen molecules.

+ Although credited as an inventor of NMR phenomenon, Rabi's experiment setup was inherently limited to basic science research and was not suitable for clinical use. Until 1940s when Felix Bloch and Edward Purcell independently discovered the NMR phenomenon in bulk materials (water and paraffin, respectively), which then paved the way for the development of NMR spectroscopy and MRI. Both Bloch and Purcell were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1952 for their discoveries.

+ Zeeman effect, discovered by Pieter Zeeman in 1896, is the splitting of spectral lines of atoms when they are subjected to an external magnetic field. The Zeeman effect provided the first indirect evidence for the existence of electron spin and its interaction with magnetic fields. Zeeman effect was a mystery that puzzled physicists for decades until 1920s when it was experimentally and then theoretically understood as a consequence of the electron's intrinsic angular momentum (spin) and its interaction with the external magnetic field. Zeeman was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1902 for his discovery.
  
+ In the effort of disproving Bohr's atom model, Stern and Gerlach carried out an experiment that provided the first direct evidence of the quantization of angular momentum in 1922. The Stern-Gerlach experiment showed that when a beam of silver atoms was passed through a non-uniform magnetic field, the atoms were deflected in two distinct directions, proving the Bohr's model was correct instead of disproving it. Stern and Gerlach sent a postcard to congratulate Bohr on the success of his model.

Albeit unknowingly at the time by every one, the implication of the Stern-Gerlach experiment was more profound and revolutionary. It did prove the quantization of angular momentum in general including the orbital angular momentum as Bohr's model predicted. But the resulting two distinct deflections did not match the predictions of Bohr's model (which predicted three deflections) and instead revealed the existence of a new type of angular momentum that is intrinsic to the particle itself, which we now know as spin. Specifically, for the spin-1/2 particles such as electrons and protons, the Stern-Gerlach experiment showed that the angular momentum can only take on two discrete values (spin up and spin down), which was a direct evidence for the existence of spin. The discovery of spin was a key milestone that paved the way for the development of quantum mechanics and our understanding of the fundamental properties of matter.

+ In 1932-1933, Stern used a similar experimental setup to measure the magnetic moment of the proton, which also has spin 1/2. Many theorists (including Pauli) advised Stern not to wasting his time since the proton's magnetic moment was known from the Dirac equation. Stern persisted and found that the proton's magnetic moment was about 3 times larger than the value predicted by the Dirac equation. This discrepancy was later explained by the discovery of the internal structure of the proton, which is made up of quarks and gluons, and the strong interactions between them. Although well-known and revolutionary, the Stern-Gerlach experiment was never recognized by the Nobel committee. Stern, however, was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1943 for his contribution to the development of the molecular ray method and his discovery of the magnetic moment of the proton.

+ In 1927, Pauli introduced three 2x2 matrices (the Pauli matrices), which were used to derive the so-called Pauli equation to describe the behavior of spin-1/2 particles and the interaction of the particle's spin with an external magnetic field. Although the Pauli equation can successfully explain the Zeeman effect, it cannot explain the fine structure of the hydrogen atom, which is a small splitting of the spectral lines of the hydrogen atom that arises from the interaction between the electron's spin and its orbital motion around the nucleus, as well as relativistic corrections to the electron's energy levels. Additionally, the Pauli equation does not provide a fundamental explanation for the origin of spin as it is a hand-crafted extension of the Schrödinger equation to include spin. Pauli was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1945 for his discovery of the Pauli exclusion principle, which states that no two fermions (particles with half-integer spin) can occupy the same quantum state simultaneously. 

+ The Dirac equation, formulated by Paul Dirac in 1928, is often considered the most fundamental description of spin-1/2 particles. Dirac's original goal was not to seek for a theoretical description of spin, but to unify quantum mechanics and Einstein's theory of special relativity. By linearizing the relativistic energy-momentum relation, Dirac derived an equation, in which the spin of the particle emerges naturally as a consequence of the relativistic effects. The Dirac equation also predicted the existence of antimatter, which was later confirmed with the discovery of the positron in 1932 by Carl Anderson. Dirac was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1933 for his discovery of new productive forms of atomic theory (i.e., quantum mechanics). Carl Anderson was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1936 for his discovery of the positron.

Bloch's and Purcell's discoveries of NMR phenomenon in bulk materials are often attributed as the key milestones that paved the way for NMR spectroscopy and MRI. However, for the quest of seeking the ultimately beautiful foundations of MRI physics, I would choose the discovery of the Dirac equation as the most fundamental milestone, because the spin concept that underlies the NMR phenomenon and MRI is beautifully and naturally emerges from the Dirac equation.

One important implication of the Dirac equation is that the Bloch equation, which describes the dynamics of the magnetization vector in MRI, can be derived from the Dirac equation under certain approximations. This connection highlights the fundamental role of the Dirac equation in MRI physics. The Bloch equations can be written as:

$$ \frac{d\mathbf{M}}{dt} = \gamma \mathbf{M} \times \mathbf{B} - \frac{M_x}{T_2} \hat{i} - \frac{M_y}{T_2} \hat{j} - \frac{M_z - M_0}{T_1} \hat{k} $$

where $\mathbf{M}$ is the magnetization vector, $\gamma$ is the gyromagnetic ratio, $\mathbf{B}$ is the external magnetic field, $T_1$ and $T_2$ are the longitudinal and transverse relaxation times, respectively, and $M_0$ is the equilibrium magnetization. The first term on the right-hand side represents the precession of the magnetization vector around the external magnetic field, which is a direct consequence of the spin dynamics described by the Dirac equation.

#### The Faraday's law of induction - the hidden gem of MRI physics

> "Nothing is too wonderful to be true, if it be consistent with the laws of nature."
> <p style="font-size: 1.0em; font-style: normal; text-align: right;">-- Michael Faraday (1791-1867) </p>

Faraday's law of induction is a fundamental principle that underlies the data acquisition process in MRI. The law states that a time-varying magnetic field induces an electromotive force (EMF) in a closed loop of wire.

The Faraday's law can be considered as a bridge that connect between the Dirac equation and the Fourier series in the context of MRI physics. The deep connection arises from the precessing magnetic moment of protons around the main magnetic field, which then induces a time-varying magnetic field that can be detected using a receiver coil (i.e., close loop of wire). The received signal has sinusoidal components, which are the basis functions of the Fourier series. The Fourier transform is then used to reconstruct the spatial distribution of the protons (i.e., the image) from the acquired signal in k-space.

## Explanations for each element

### Fourier series - the mathematical poem

> "Fourier's great mathematical poem"
> <p style="font-size: 1.0em; font-style: normal; text-align: right;">-- Lord Kelvin (William Thomson) (1824-1907) </p>

In 1822, Joseph Fourier published his book "Théorie de la chaleur" (The Theory of Heat), in which he introduced the concept of representing a periodic function as a sum of sine and cosine functions, now known as the Fourier series. Concretely, Fourier showed that for any periodic function $f(x)$ with period $2\pi$, it can be expressed as an infinite sum of sine and cosine functions:

$$ f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + a_1 \cos(x) + b_1 \sin(x) + a_2 \cos(2x) + b_2 \sin(2x) + \cdots $$

or

$$ f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \bigg[a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx)\bigg] $$

where $a_0$, $a_n$, and $b_n$ are the Fourier coefficients that can be calculated using the following formulas:

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) \, dx $$

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) \cos(nx) \, dx $$

$$ b_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) \sin(nx) \, dx $$

Using Euler's formula:

$$ e^{ix} = \cos(x) + i\sin(x)$$

$$ e^{-ix} = \cos(x) - i\sin(x)$$

we can express the sine and cosine functions in terms of complex exponentials:

$$ \cos(x) = \frac{e^{ix} + e^{-ix}}{2} $$

$$ \sin(x) = \frac{e^{ix} - e^{-ix}}{2i} $$

then the Fourier series can also be expressed in terms of complex exponentials:

$$ f(x) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n e^{i n x} $$

where the complex Fourier coefficients $c_n$ are given by:

$$ c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) e^{-i n x} \, dx $$

The Fourier series is a foundation for the development of the Fourier transform, the discrete Fourier transform (DFT), and the fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm for efficient DFT, which are fundamental tools in signal processing, image processing, and many other fields in science and engineering, including MRI physics.

The Fourier transform is instrumental for theoretical understanding of MRI physics while the FFT algorithm is essential for practical implementation of MRI image reconstruction.

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is described as:

> "The most valuable numerical algorithm in our lifetime."
> <p style="font-size: 1.0em; font-style: normal; text-align: right;">-- Gilbert Strang (1934-)</p>

It also listed as one of the top 10 algorithms by the IEEE magazine Computing in Science & Engineering in 2000.

### The Dirac equation - the physics poem
> "How did you find the Dirac equation? Professor Dirac?"
> <p style="font-size: 1.0em; font-style: italic; text-align: right;">"I found it beautiful."</p>
> <p style="font-size: 1.0em; font-style: normal; text-align: right;">-- Sir Michael Berry, "Paul Dirac: the purest soul in physics", Feb 01, 1998 </p>

#### The Schrödinger equation

For general audience, the Schrödinger equation is often referred as the fundamental equation of quantum mechanics. 

$$ i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi(\mathbf{r}, t) = \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 + V(\mathbf{r}) \right]\psi(\mathbf{r}, t) $$

where $\psi$ is the wave function of the quantum system, $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, $m$ is the mass of the particle, $\nabla^2$ is the Laplacian operator representing the kinetic energy, and $V(\mathbf{r})$ is the potential energy as a function of position.

Despite its fundamental role in quantum mechanics, the Schrödinger equation is unable to explain the spectral line splitting observed in the Zeeman effect, which is a direct consequence of the electron's spin. As a matter of fact, the Schrödinger did initially attempt to formulate a realtivistic wave equation for a particle using the relativistic energy-momentum relation, but what he obtained is essentially what is now called the Klein-Gordon equation for a spin-0 relativistic particle in a Coulomb potential. He found the standing waves solutions but the relativistic solutions did not agreed with the observed fine structure of the hydrogen atom. Discouraged, he abandoned the relativistic wave equation and published the non-relativistic wave equation that we now know as the Schrödinger equation in 1926. Schrödinger was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1933 for his discovery of new productive forms of atomic theory (i.e., quantum mechanics) along with Paul Dirac.

To insert the spin into the wave equation, Pauli introduced three 2-by-2 matrices to hand-crafted the Pauli equation, which describes the behavior of spin-1/2 particles in an external magnetic field. The Pauli equation can be written as: 

$$ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi\rangle = \left[\frac{1}{2m}\left(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A\right)^2- q\hbar\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\mathbf B+ q\phi\right]|\psi\rangle$$

where $\|\psi\rangle$ is the two-component spinor wave function, $\hat{\mathbf p}$ is the momentum operator, $q$ is the charge of the particle, $\mathbf A$ is the vector potential, $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ are the Pauli matrices representing the spin operators, $\mathbf B$ is the magnetic field, and $\phi$ is the scalar potential.

#### The Pauli equation

The Pauli equation can describe the Zeeman effect but not the fine structure of the hydrogen atom, which is a small splitting of the spectral lines of the hydrogen atom that arises from the interaction between the electron's spin and its orbital motion around the nucleus, as well as relativistic corrections to the electron's energy levels.

Additionally, the Schrödinger equation and the Pauli equation can describe the behavior electrons in atoms with small atomic numbers, but they fail to accurately describe the behavior of electrons in atoms with large atomic numbers (such as gold) due to the significant relativistic effects that arise from the high nuclear charge. The color of gold is a consequence of the relativistic effects on the electrons in gold atoms, which leads to a shift in the energy levels and results in the characteristic yellow color of gold. If there is no shift in the energy levels, gold would appear silvery white like silver as predicted by the Schrödinger equation and the Pauli equation.

#### The Klein-Gordon equation 

Before Dirac, Klein-Gordon equation was the first attempt to formulate a relativistic wave equation, which is a differential equation version of the Einstein's energy-momentum relation: 

$$ E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4 \rightarrow \frac{E^2}{c^2} - \mathbf{p}^2 - m^2 c^2 = 0$$

applying the quantum mechanical operators for energy and momentum:

$$ E \rightarrow i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} $$

$$ \mathbf{p} \rightarrow -i\hbar \nabla $$

we can derive the Klein-Gordon equation:

$$ \left(\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 + \frac{m^2 c^2}{\hbar^2}\right) \psi = 0 $$


Unlike the Schrödinger equation, the Klein-Gordon equation is a second-order differential equation in time, so to find a definite solution, we need to specify both the initial wave function and its time derivative, which is not the case for the Schrödinger equation. Second, the Klein-Gordon equation leads to negative probability densities, which cannot be interpreted as physical probabilities. Third, the Klein-Gordon equation admits solutions with negative energy, which was a puzzle.

#### The Dirac equation

Dissatisfied with Klein-Gordon equation, Dirac was originally motivated to formulate a relativistic wave equation that would unify quantum mechanics and Einstein's theory of special relativity, in which the particle is moving at speeds close to the speed of light.

Dirac's insight was to linearize the relativistic energy-momentum relation, which led to the Dirac equation, which solved the first two problems of the Klein-Gordon equation and provided an explanation for the negative energy solutions as the existence of antimatter. Start from the relativistic energy-momentum relation:

$$ E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4 \rightarrow \frac{E}{c} = \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 + m^2 c^2} $$

Expressing the momentum vector $\mathbf{p}$ in terms of its components:

$$ \frac{E}{c} = \sqrt{p_x^2 + p_y^2 + p_z^2 + (mc)^2} $$

Dirac's idea was to find a set of matrices $\alpha_i$ for $i=x,y,z$ and $\beta$ such that the following equation holds:

$$ \sqrt{p_x^2 + p_y^2 + p_z^2 + p_0^2}  = \alpha_0 mc + \alpha_x p_x + \alpha_y p_y + \alpha_z p_z $$

By squaring both sides of the equation and balancing the like terms, we can derive the following conditions for the matrices $\alpha_i$ and $\beta$:

$$ \alpha_i^2 = I $$

$$ \alpha_i \alpha_j + \alpha_j \alpha_i = 0 \quad \text{for } i \neq j $$

$$ \beta^2 = I $$

$$ \beta \alpha_i + \alpha_i \beta = 0 $$

Apparently, no number would satisfy the above condition. Instead, three 2x2 Pauli matrices ($\sigma^1$, $\sigma^2$, $\sigma^3$) can satisfy the above conditions:

$$ \sigma^x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma^y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma^z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} $$

but the $\beta$ is non-existent. Dirac found that one needs not to restrict oneself in the 2x2 matrices, one can use 4x4 matrices to satisfy all the conditions, which leads to the following representation of the $\alpha_i$ and $\beta$ matrices:

$$ \beta = \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ 0 & -I \end{pmatrix}, \quad \alpha_i = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sigma^i \\ \sigma^i & 0 \end{pmatrix} $$

Substituting the above $\alpha_i$ matrices into the linearized energy-momentum relation, we get:

$$ \frac{E}{c} = \beta mc + \alpha_x p_x + \alpha_y p_y + \alpha_z p_z $$

Rewriting the above equation in a more compact form, we have:

$$ E = \beta mc^2 + c\boldsymbol{\alpha} \cdot \mathbf{p} $$

Applying the quantum mechanical operators for energy and momentum, we can derive the Dirac equation in the following form:

$$ i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} |\psi\rangle = \bigg[\beta mc^2 -i\hbar c \boldsymbol{\alpha} \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla}\bigg] |\psi\rangle $$

where $\|\psi\rangle$ is a four-component spinor wave function. The top two components of the spinor correspond to the particle (electron) solutions, while the bottom two components correspond to the antiparticle (positron) solutions. The Dirac equation successfully describes the behavior of spin-1/2 particles and their interactions with electromagnetic fields, and it is a cornerstone of quantum field theory of the electron.

##### Dirac equation in natural units and Feynman slash notation

To transform the Dirac equation into a more elegant and compact form, we can introduce the gamma matrices $\gamma^\mu$ defined as:

$$ \gamma^0 = \beta $$

$$ \gamma^1 = \beta \alpha_x, \quad \gamma^2 = \beta \alpha_y, \quad \gamma^3 = \beta \alpha_z $$

Pre-multiplying the Dirac equation by $\beta$, we have:

$$ i\hbar \beta \frac{\partial}{\partial t} |\psi\rangle = \bigg[\beta^2 mc^2 -i\hbar c \beta \boldsymbol{\alpha} \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla}\bigg] |\psi\rangle $$

Rearranging the above equation and note that $\beta^2 = I$, we get:

$$ \left(i\hbar \beta \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + i\hbar c \beta \boldsymbol{\alpha} \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla} - mc^2\right) |\psi\rangle = 0 $$

In the natural units where $\hbar = c = 1$, the above equation can be written in the following form:

$$ \left(i \gamma^0 \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + i \boldsymbol{\gamma} \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla} - m\right) |\psi\rangle = 0 $$

Let's define the four-vector derivative operator $\partial_\mu$ as:

$$ \partial_\mu = \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}, \boldsymbol{\nabla}\right) $$

Then the Dirac equation can be written in the following compact form:

$$ \left(i\gamma^\mu \partial_\mu - m\right) |\psi\rangle = 0 $$

where $\gamma^\mu \partial_\mu$ is a shorthand notation (i.e., Einstein summation convention) for the following expression:

$$\gamma^\mu \partial_\mu = \gamma^0 \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \gamma^1 \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \gamma^2 \frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \gamma^3 \frac{\partial}{\partial z}$$

Using Feynman slash notation, we can further simplify the above equation to the following form:

$$ \left(i\cancel{\partial} - m\right) |\psi\rangle = 0 $$

where $\cancel{\partial} = \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu$ is the Feynman slash notation for the four-vector derivative operator.

In the presence of an external electromagnetic field, the Dirac equation can be modified by replacing the energy and momentum operators with their corresponding gauge-covariant derivatives:

$$ E \rightarrow E - q\phi $$

$$ \mathbf{p} \rightarrow \mathbf{p} - q\mathbf{A} $$

where $q$ is the charge of the particle, $\phi$ is the scalar potential, and $\mathbf{A}$ is the vector potential. The modified Dirac equation in the presence of an external electromagnetic field can be written as:

$$ \left[i\gamma^\mu \left(\partial_\mu -q A_\mu\right) - m\right] |\psi\rangle = 0 $$

where $A_\mu = (\phi, \mathbf{A})$ is the four-vector potential of the electromagnetic field. Let's define:

$$ D_\mu = \partial_\mu - q A_\mu $$

Then the Dirac equation in the presence of an external electromagnetic field can be written in the following compact form:

$$ \left(i\gamma^\mu D_\mu - m\right) |\psi\rangle = 0 $$

Similar to the case without an external electromagnetic field, we can also write the above equation in the Feynman slash notation as:

$$ \left(i\cancel{D} - m\right) |\psi\rangle = 0 $$

where $\cancel{D} = \gamma^\mu D_\mu$ is the Feynman slash notation for the gauge-covariant derivative operator.

#### From Dirac equation to Pauli equation

Let's start with the linearized form of the Dirac equation:

$$ i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} |\psi\rangle = \bigg[\beta mc^2 -i\hbar c \boldsymbol{\alpha} \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla}\bigg] |\psi\rangle $$

Now, let's include the interaction with an external electromagnetic field by replacing the energy and momentum operators with their corresponding gauge-covariant derivatives:

$$ E \rightarrow E - q\phi $$

$$ \mathbf{p} \rightarrow \mathbf{p} - q\mathbf{A} $$

$$ i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} |\psi\rangle = \bigg[c \boldsymbol{\alpha} \cdot \left(\hat{\mathbf{p}} - q\mathbf{A}\right) + \beta mc^2 + q\phi\bigg] |\psi\rangle $$

where $q$ is the charge of the particle, $\phi$ is the scalar potential, $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ is the momentum operator, and $\mathbf{A}$ is the vector potential of the electromagnetic field.

Let's express the four-component spinor wave function $\|\psi\rangle$ in terms of its upper and lower two-component spinors:

$$
|\psi\rangle
=
e^{-i m c^2 t/\hbar}
\begin{pmatrix}
\varphi_1\\
\varphi_2\\
\chi_1\\
\chi_2
\end{pmatrix}
=
e^{-i m c^2 t/\hbar}
\begin{pmatrix}
\varphi\\
\chi
\end{pmatrix},
% \tag{4}
$$

Substituting the above expression for $\|\psi\rangle$ into the modified Dirac equation, we can derive the following coupled equations for the upper and lower two-component spinors $\varphi$ and $\chi$:

$$
i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
\begin{pmatrix}
\varphi\\
\chi
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
q\phi &
c\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A)\\
c\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A) &
q\phi-2mc^2
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\varphi\\
\chi
\end{pmatrix},
% \tag{5}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3)$ are the Pauli matrices representing the spin operators.

Let's expand the above equation into two separate equations for $\varphi$ and $\chi$:

$$
i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi = q\phi \varphi + c\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A) \chi
$$

$$
i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\chi = c\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A) \varphi + (q\phi - 2mc^2) \chi
$$

Let's rearrange the second equation to have $\chi$ on the left-hand side:

$$
\bigg[i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t} - q\phi + 2mc^2\bigg] \chi = c\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A) \varphi
$$

For the non-relativistic limit the $2mc^2$ term is much larger than the other terms, so the above equation can be approximated as:

$$
2mc^2 \chi \approx c\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A) \varphi
$$

which gives us the following expression for $\chi$:

$$
\chi \approx \frac{1}{2mc} \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A) \varphi
$$

Substituting the above expression for $\chi$ back into the first equation, we obtain the following equation for $\varphi$, a non-relativistic limit of the Dirac equation:

$$
i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi = q\phi \varphi + \frac{1}{2m} \left[\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A)\right]^2 \varphi
$$

Expanding the square term and using the identity for the Pauli matrices:

$$
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{a}) \, (\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} + i \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot (\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b})
$$

we have:

$$
\left[\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A)\right]^2 = (\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A)^2 + i q\boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \big[(\hat{\mathbf p} - q \mathbf A) \times (\hat{\mathbf p} - q \mathbf A)\big]
$$

Note that unlike a vector, the differential operator
$\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A=-i\hbar\nabla-q\mathbf A$
has non-zero cross product with itself. This can be seen by considering the cross product applied to a scalar function $\psi$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bigg[(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A)\times(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A)\bigg]\psi
&=-q\bigg[\hat{\mathbf p}\times(\mathbf A\psi)+\mathbf A\times(\hat{\mathbf p}\psi)\bigg] \\
&=iq\hbar\bigg[\nabla\times(\mathbf A\psi)+\mathbf A\times(\nabla\psi)\bigg] \\
&=iq\hbar\bigg[\psi(\nabla\times\mathbf A)-\mathbf A\times(\nabla\psi)+\mathbf A\times(\nabla\psi)\bigg] \\
&=iq\hbar\,\mathbf B\,\psi
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$ is the magnetic field.

Substituting the above expression back into the equation for $\varphi$, we get:

$$
i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi = q\phi \varphi + \frac{1}{2m} (\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A)^2 \varphi - \frac{q\hbar}{2m} \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf B \varphi
$$

This is the Pauli equation! So the spin is naturally emerges from the non-relativistic limit of the Dirac equation, and the interaction between the spin and the magnetic field is described by the term $-\frac{q\hbar}{2m} \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf B$ in the Pauli equation.

Now let's consider the case of constant and uniform magnetic field $\mathbf B$, the vector potential $\mathbf A$ can be chosen in the symmetric gauge as:

$$\mathbf A = \frac{1}{2} \mathbf B \times \mathbf r$$

Let's expand the square term in the above equation:

$$
(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A)^2 = \hat{\mathbf p}^2 - q (\hat{\mathbf p} \cdot \mathbf A + \mathbf A \cdot \hat{\mathbf p}) + q^2 \mathbf A^2
$$

Substituting the vector potential $\mathbf A$ in the symmetric gauge into the above equation, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\hat{\mathbf p}-q\mathbf A)^2 &= \hat{\mathbf p}^2 - \frac{q}{2} \bigg[\hat{\mathbf p} \cdot (\mathbf B \times \mathbf r) + (\mathbf B \times \mathbf r) \cdot \hat{\mathbf p}\bigg] + \frac{q^2}{4} (\mathbf B \times \mathbf r)^2 \\
&= \hat{\mathbf p}^2 - \frac{q}{2} \bigg[\hat{\mathbf p} \cdot (\mathbf B \times \mathbf r) + (\mathbf B \times \mathbf r) \cdot \hat{\mathbf p}\bigg] + \frac{q^2}{4} (\mathbf B \times \mathbf r)^2 \\
&= \hat{\mathbf p}^2 - q(\mathbf{r} \times \hat{\mathbf p}) \cdot \mathbf{B} + \frac{q^2}{4} (\mathbf B \times \mathbf r)^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the above expression back into the Pauli equation, we get:

$$
i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi = q\phi \varphi + \frac{1}{2m} \hat{\mathbf p}^2 \varphi - \frac{q}{2m} \mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{B} \varphi + \frac{q^2}{8m} (\mathbf B \times \mathbf r)^2 \varphi - \frac{q\hbar}{2m} \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf B \varphi
$$  

where $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \hat{\mathbf p}$ is the orbital angular momentum operator.


#### From Pauli equation to the Bloch equation

For MRI physics, we will only care about the spin dynamics of the protons in the presence of a magnetic field, so we can ignore the kinetic energy term and the scalar potential term in the Pauli equation, which gives us the following simplified equation:

$$i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi = - \frac{q}{2m} \mathbf{L} \cdot \mathbf{B} \varphi + \frac{q^2}{8m} \bigg[B^2 r^2 - (\mathbf B \cdot \mathbf r)^2\bigg] \varphi - \frac{q\hbar}{2m} \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf B \varphi$$

For a proton, the orbital angular momentum is approximately zero, so the first term on the right-hand side can be ignored. Additionally, the second term on the right-hand side is a constant energy shift that does not affect the spin dynamics, so it can also be ignored. Therefore, we are left with the following equation that describes the spin dynamics of a proton in an external magnetic field:

$$i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi = - \frac{q\hbar}{2m} \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf B \varphi$$

Let's define the spin operator as $\boldsymbol{S} = \frac{\hbar}{2} \boldsymbol{\sigma}$, then the above equation can be rewritten as:

$$i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi = - \gamma \mathbf{S} \cdot \mathbf B \varphi$$

where $\gamma = \frac{q}{m}$ is the gyromagnetic ratio of the proton as a predicted by the Dirac equation. However, the experimentally measured gyromagnetic ratio of the proton is approximately 2.79 times larger than the predicted value, which is a consequence of the strong interaction between the quarks inside the proton that is not accounted for in the Dirac equation. The correct value of the gyromagnetic ratio of the proton is:

$$\gamma_p = g_p \frac{q}{2m}$$

where $g_p \approx 5.585$ is the proton g-factor. The discrepancy between the predicted and measured gyromagnetic ratio of the proton is a reminder that the Dirac equation is a relativistic quantum mechanical equation for point-like particles, and it does not account for the internal structure of composite particles like protons.

Let's use the experimentally measured gyromagnetic ratio of the proton to rewrite the above equation as:

$$i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varphi = - \gamma_p \boldsymbol{S} \cdot \mathbf B \varphi$$

Hence, the Hamiltonian for the spin dynamics of a proton in an external magnetic field can be written as:

$$\hat{H}_{spin} = - \gamma_p \boldsymbol{S} \cdot \mathbf B$$

The magnetic moment operator is defined as $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \gamma_p \boldsymbol{S}$, so the above Hamiltonian can also be written as:

$$\hat{H}_{spin} = - \boldsymbol{\mu} \cdot \mathbf B$$

This has a form of the classical potential energy of a magnetic dipole in a field, but here it is derived fundamentally from the Dirac equation. The above Hamiltonian describes the interaction between the magnetic moment of the proton and the external magnetic field.

Now, let's rewrite the Hamiltonian in terms of the $x,y,z$ components of the spin operator:

$$
H = -\gamma_p \mathbf{S} \cdot \mathbf{B} = -\gamma_p (S_x B_x + S_y B_y + S_z B_z)
$$

The Heisenberg equation of motion for an operator $A$ (assuming $A$ is not explicitly time-dependent) is given by:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} \langle A \rangle = \frac{i}{\hbar} \langle [H, A] \rangle
$$

Applying the Heisenberg equation of motion to the x-component of the spin, $S_x$:

$$
\frac{d S_x}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar} [-\gamma_p (S_x B_x + S_y B_y + S_z B_z), S_x]
$$

Using the standard angular momentum commutation relations $[S_i, S_j] = i\hbar \epsilon_{ijk} S_k$:

$$[S_x, S_x] = 0$$

$$[S_y, S_x] = -[S_x, S_y] = -i\hbar S_z$$

$$[S_z, S_x] = i\hbar S_y$$

Substituting these back into the equation for $S_x$:

$$
\frac{d S_x}{dt} = -\frac{i\gamma_p}{\hbar} (B_y [S_y, S_x] + B_z [S_z, S_x])
$$

$$
\frac{d S_x}{dt} = -\frac{i\gamma_p}{\hbar} (B_y (-i\hbar S_z) + B_z (i\hbar S_y))
$$

$$
\frac{d S_x}{dt} = -\gamma_p (B_y S_z - B_z S_y) = \gamma_p (S_y B_z - S_z B_y)
$$

Similarly, we can compute the derivatives for $S_y$ and $S_z$:

$$
\frac{d S_y}{dt} = \gamma_p (S_z B_x - S_x B_z)
$$

$$
\frac{d S_z}{dt} = \gamma_p (S_x B_y - S_y B_x)
$$

Combining the above three equations, we can write the time evolution of the spin operator in vector form as:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}
S_x \\
S_y \\
S_z
\end{pmatrix}
=
\gamma_p
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & B_z & -B_y \\
-B_z & 0 & B_x \\
B_y & -B_x & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
S_x \\
S_y \\
S_z
\end{pmatrix}
$$

The above equation can be rewritten in a more compact form using the cross product:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{S}}{dt} = \gamma_p \mathbf{S} \times \mathbf{B}
$$

##### Macroscopic magnetization and the Bloch equation

The macroscopic nuclear magnetization $\mathbf{M}$ is the sum of the magnetic moments per unit volume. If there are $N$ spins:

$$
\mathbf{M} = \sum \mathbf{\mu}_i = N \gamma_p \langle \mathbf{S} \rangle
$$

Multiplying the single-spin equation by $N\gamma_p$, the so-called Bloch equation:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{M}}{dt} = \gamma_p \mathbf{M} \times \mathbf{B}
$$

##### Larmor precession as a solution to the Bloch equation

Suppose the spin system is exposed to a static magnetic field $\vec{B} = B_0 \hat{k}$ aligned with the z-axis. The Bloch equation can be written as:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{M}}{dt} = \gamma_p (\mathbf{M} \times B_0 \hat{k})
$$

Expanding the cross product into Cartesian components yields a system of three linear first-order differential equations:

$$
\frac{dM_x}{dt} = \gamma_p B_0 M_y = \omega_0 M_y
$$

$$
\frac{dM_y}{dt} = -\gamma_p B_0 M_x = -\omega_0 M_x
$$

$$
\frac{dM_z}{dt} = 0
$$

where $\omega_0 = \gamma_p B_0$ is the **Larmor Frequency**.

The equation for the z-component is uncoupled and trivial to integrate:

$$
\frac{dM_z}{dt} = 0 \implies M_z(t) = M_{z0}
$$

The equations for $M_x$ and $M_y$ are coupled. To solve them, we differentiate the first equation with respect to time:

$$
\frac{d^2 M_x}{dt^2} = \omega_0 \frac{dM_y}{dt}
$$

Substitute the expression for $dM_y/dt$:

$$
\frac{d^2 M_x}{dt^2} = \omega_0 (-\omega_0 M_x) = -\omega_0^2 M_x
$$

This is the standard equation for a simple harmonic oscillator. The general solution is:

$$
M_x(t) = A \cos(\omega_0 t) + B \sin(\omega_0 t)
$$

We find $M_y(t)$ by substituting this back into the first order equation $M_y = \frac{1}{\omega_0} \frac{dM_x}{dt}$:

$$
M_y(t) = -A \sin(\omega_0 t) + B \cos(\omega_0 t)
$$

Assuming that at time $t=0$, the magnetization vector is $\mathbf{M}(0) = (M_{x0}, M_{y0}, M_{z0})$, we can determine the constants $A$ and $B$:

$$
\begin{cases}
M_x(0) = A \cos(0) + B \sin(0) = A \\
M_y(0) = -A \sin(0) + B \cos(0) = B \\
M_z(0) = M_{z0}
\end{cases}
$$

Therefore, $A = M_{x0}$ and $B = M_{y0}$. Substituting these back into the solutions for $M_x(t)$ and $M_y(t)$, we get:

$$
M_x(t) = M_{x0} \cos(\omega_0 t) + M_{y0} \sin(\omega_0 t)
$$

$$
M_y(t) = -M_{x0} \sin(\omega_0 t) + M_{y0} \cos(\omega_0 t)
$$

Rearranging the above equations into matrix form, we have:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} M_x(t) \\ M_y(t) \\ M_z(t) \end{pmatrix} =
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos(\omega_0 t) & \sin(\omega_0 t) & 0 \\
-\sin(\omega_0 t) & \cos(\omega_0 t) & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} M_x(0) \\ M_y(0) \\ M_z(0) \end{pmatrix}
$$

or equivalently:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} M_x(t) \\ M_y(t) \\ M_z(t) \end{pmatrix} =
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos(\omega_0 t) & \sin(\omega_0 t) & 0 \\
-\sin(\omega_0 t) & \cos(\omega_0 t) & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} M_{x0} \\ M_{y0} \\ M_{z0} \end{pmatrix}
$$


This matrix describes a left-handed rotation (clockwise) about the z-axis. The magnetization vector precesses around the z-axis at the Larmor frequency $\omega_0 = \gamma_p B_0$, maintaining a constant magnitude in the transverse plane.


##### Relaxation effects in the Bloch equation

To account for the relaxation effects due to energy loss ($T_1$) and decoherence ($T_2$), the full Bloch equation includes relaxation terms:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{M}}{dt} = \gamma_p (\mathbf{M} \times \mathbf{B}) - \frac{M_x \hat{i} + M_y \hat{j}}{T_2} - \frac{(M_z - M_0) \hat{k}}{T_1}
$$

where $M_0$ is the equilibrium magnetization along the z-axis and $\hat{i}, \hat{j}, \hat{k}$ are the unit vectors in the x, y, and z directions respectively. This equation describes the dynamics of the macroscopic magnetization in an external magnetic field, including both precession and relaxation effects.

#### Fine structure of the hydrogen atom - Explained

From the Dirac equation, we can derive the spin-orbital coupling term, which can explain the fine structure of the hydrogen atom. The spin-orbital coupling arises from the interaction between the electron's spin and its orbital motion around the nucleus.

The linearized form of the Dirac equation for an electron in a Coulomb potential of proton in the hydrogen atom can be written as: 

$$
E |\psi\rangle = \bigg[c \boldsymbol{\alpha} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{p}} + \beta mc^2 +V\bigg] |\psi\rangle
$$

where $e$ is the charge of the electron, $V = -\frac{e^2}{r}$ is the Coulomb potential in atomic units, and $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ is the momentum operator, and $E$ is the total energy of the system.

Let's represent the four-component spinor wave function $\|\psi\rangle$ in terms of its upper and lower two-component spinors:

$$
|\psi\rangle
=\begin{pmatrix}
\varphi\\
\chi
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Substituting the above expression for $\|\psi\rangle$ into the linearized Dirac equation, we can derive the following coupled equations for the upper and lower two-component spinors $\varphi$ and $\chi$:

$$
\bigg(E - V - mc^2 \bigg) \varphi = c\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} \chi
$$  

$$
\bigg(E - V + mc^2 \bigg) \chi = c\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} \varphi
$$

The second equation can be rearranged to express $\chi$ in terms of $\varphi$:

$$
\chi = \frac{1}{E - V + mc^2} c\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} \varphi
$$

Substituting the second equation into the first equation, we can derive the following equation for $\varphi$:

$$
\bigg(E - V - mc^2 \bigg) \varphi = c\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p}\left[\frac{1}{E - V + mc^2}\right] c\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} \varphi
$$

Let's call $E_S = E - mc^2$ the Schrödinger energy the equation can be rewritten as:

$$
\bigg(E_S - V \bigg) \varphi = c^2\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p}\left[\frac{1}{E_S - V + 2mc^2}\right] \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} \varphi
$$

The expression in the square brackets can be expanded as a power series in $\frac{1}{2mc^2}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{E_S - V + 2mc^2} &= \frac{1}{2mc^2} \frac{1}{1 + \frac{E_S - V}{2mc^2}} \\
&= \frac{1}{2mc^2} \left[1 - \frac{E_S - V}{2mc^2} + \left(\frac{E_S - V}{2mc^2}\right)^2 - \cdots \right]
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's only keep the first two terms in the above expansion, which gives us the following approximation:

$$
\frac{1}{E_S - V + 2mc^2} \approx \frac{1}{2mc^2} \left[1 - \frac{E_S - V}{2mc^2}\right]
$$

Substituting the above approximation back into the equation for $\varphi$, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bigg(E_S - V \bigg) \varphi &\approx c^2\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p}\left[\frac{1}{2mc^2} \left(1 - \frac{E_S - V}{2mc^2}\right)\right] \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} \varphi \\
&= \bigg[\frac{1}{2m} \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} - \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p}(E_S - V) \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} }{4m^2 c^2} \bigg]\varphi \\
&= \bigg[\frac{1}{2m} \hat{\mathbf p}^2 - \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} E_S \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p}}{4m^2 c^2} + \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} V \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} }{4m^2 c^2}\bigg]\varphi
\end{aligned}
$$

Rewriting the above equation, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_S \varphi &\approx \bigg[\frac{1}{2m} \hat{\mathbf p}^2  + V- \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} E_S \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p}}{4m^2 c^2} + \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} V \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} }{4m^2 c^2}\bigg]\varphi
\end{aligned}
$$

The third term on the right-hand side of the above equation is a relativistic correction to the kinetic energy since we treat $E_S$ separately from the Coulomb potential $V$, hence $E_S = \frac{1}{2m} \hat{\mathbf p}^2$, so we can write the above equation as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_S \varphi &\approx \bigg[\frac{\hat{\mathbf p}^2}{2m}   + V- \frac{\hat{\mathbf p}^4}{8m^3 c^2}  + \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} V \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} }{4m^2 c^2}\bigg]\varphi
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\mathbf{p} = -i\hbar \nabla$ is a differential operator, it acts on both the wave function $\varphi$ and the potential $V$. Therefore we have:

$$
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} V = V (\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p}) - i\hbar (\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\nabla V)
$$

Substituting the above expression to the last term in the above equation, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H_\text{4} &= \frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} V \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} }{4m^2 c^2} \\
&= \frac{1}{4m^2 c^2} \bigg[V \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p} - i\hbar (\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\nabla V) \boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p}\bigg] \\
&= \frac{1}{4m^2 c^2} \bigg[V \hat{\mathbf p}^2 - i\hbar (\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\nabla V) (\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p})\bigg]
\end{aligned}
$$

Use Pauli matrix identity again, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\nabla V) (\boldsymbol{\sigma}\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf p}) &= \nabla V \cdot \hat{\mathbf p} + i \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot (\nabla V \times \hat{\mathbf p}) \\
&= \nabla V \cdot \hat{\mathbf p} + i \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \bigg(\frac{1}{r} \frac{dV}{dr} \mathbf{r} \times \hat{\mathbf p}\bigg) \\
&= \nabla V \cdot \hat{\mathbf p} + i\frac{1}{r} \frac{dV}{dr} \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{L}
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the above expression back into the equation for $H_4$, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H_\text{4} &= \frac{1}{4m^2 c^2} \bigg[V \hat{\mathbf p}^2 - i\hbar \nabla V \cdot \hat{\mathbf p} + \hbar \frac{1}{r} \frac{dV}{dr} \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{L}\bigg] \\
&= \frac{V \hat{\mathbf p}^2}{4m^2 c^2} - \frac{i\hbar \nabla V \cdot \hat{\mathbf p}}{4m^2 c^2} + \frac{\hbar}{4m^2 c^2} \bigg[\frac{1}{r} \frac{dV}{dr}\bigg] \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{L}
\end{aligned}
$$

The spin-orbital coupling term is the second term on the right-hand side of the above equation:

$$
H_\text{SO} = \frac{\hbar}{4m^2 c^2} \bigg[\frac{1}{r} \frac{dV}{dr}\bigg] \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{L}
$$

Substituting the Coulomb potential $V = -\frac{e^2}{r}$ into the above equation, and use the definition of spin operator $\mathbf{S} = \frac{\hbar}{2} \boldsymbol{\sigma}$, we have:

$$
H_\text{SO} = \frac{1}{2m^2 c^2} \bigg[\frac{1}{r} \frac{d}{dr}\bigg(-\frac{e^2}{r}\bigg)\bigg] \bigg(\frac{\hbar}{2} \boldsymbol{\sigma}\bigg) \cdot \mathbf{L} = \frac{e^2}{2m^2 c^2 r^3} \mathbf{S} \cdot \mathbf{L}
$$

The Darwin term is the second term on the right-hand side of the equation for $H_4$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H_\text{Darwin} &= - \frac{i\hbar \nabla V \cdot \hat{\mathbf p}}{4m^2 c^2} \\
&= \frac{\hbar^2}{4m^2 c^2} \nabla V \cdot \nabla \\
\end{aligned}
$$

We have:

$$
\int u\,(-\nabla V \cdot \nabla u)\, dx
=
\frac{1}{2}
\int (\Delta V)\,u^2\, dx .
$$

Therefore, the Darwin term can be written as:

$$
H_\text{Darwin} = \frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2 c^2} \Delta V
$$

The spin-orbit coupling term together with the Darwin term and the relativistic correction to the kinetic energy term can explain the fine structure of the energy levels of the hydrogen atom.

I was specialized in theoretical physics from my undergraduate to PhD studies, so when I decided to pursue my PhD research on MRI physics, I was initially confident that I would be able to easily understand the physics of MRI given its quantum mechanical origins. However, I quickly realized that majority of the MRI physics can be understood without any knowledge of quantum mechanics, except some of specialized topics of NMR spectroscopy. However, a strong foundation in quantum mechanics allows me to appreciate the beauty and elegance of the underlying physics of MRI, in a way similar to a bilingual person can appreciate the beauty of a poem in its original language, which may be lost in translation. 

As a physicist by training, I cannot help but to list many quatum mechanical concepts and events that are related to the Dirac equation. However, this probably one of the very few times in the AoMP series that we will talk about quantum mechanics and the Dirac equation.

You don't need to know any quantum mechanics leave a lone the Dirac equation to fully understand the basic principles of MRI. However, quantum mechanics and the Dirac equation and quantum mechanics are beutiful and historically important for the development of MRI. That why I dedicated this post to the history of the Dirac equation and its connection to MRI physics.
