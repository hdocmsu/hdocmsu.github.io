### Remarks

This project was motivated by the *mysterious* artifact that I encountered when I first *re-implemented* the CG-SENSE algorithm.

I have been working on translating ultrashort echo time (UTE) sequence to the clinical environment for many years and are interested shortening its scan time. Conjugate Gradient SENSE (CG-SENSE) is a natural choice because of the non-Cartesian sampling trajectories used in UTE sequences.

When I first re-implemented CG-SENSE based on [Maier et al., MRM 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/mrm.20648) CG-SENSE's github repo, I encountered the *"halo"* artifact using a provided gridding tutorial. After a bit of investigation, I found that the density correction was not applied to the k-space data before gridding.

The *"halo"* artifact was not so surprising because of its recognizable low-frequency nature, which is linked to the over-sampling of the k-space center in the radial k-space trajectory. After fixing the typo, the *"halo"* artifact was resolved, but then I encountered a *"mysterious"* artifact that I could not explain.

The mysterious artifact was the *"subtle"* noise amplification in the CG-SENSE reconstructed images.

+ My first instinct was to increase the number of iterations hoping that it would reduce the noise amplification, but that only made the artifact worse.

+ Second, I thought it might be a bug in the given implementation of conjugate gradient algorithm, but after carefully checking the given code and re-implementing a few variants of the conjugate gradient algorithm, I was able to rule out that possibility.

+ Third, I thought it might be a problem associated with the conjugate gradient algorithm, so I implemented the naive and optimized gradient descent algorithms, but the artifact persisted. 

+ Fourth, I inspected intermediate reconstructed images at every iteration and its residual norm, upon which I found that the residual norm was decreasing smoothly at the beginning, but then it started to fluctuate widely at later iterations. I suspected there was a problem with the convergence behavior, but I still could not explain why.

+ Finally, during the research process, I found [Qu et al., MRM 2005](https://doi.org/10.1002/mrm.20648) and learned about the concept of *"semi-convergence"* in iterative reconstruction algorithms. 

The semi-convergence phenomenon is a well-known behavior in iterative reconstruction algorithms in mathematics and computer science, in which image quality initially improves with increasing iterations, but subsequently deteriorates beyond a certain point. This effect arises from the ill-posed nature of inverse problems, such as image reconstruction from undersampled data. 

In the case of CG-SENSE reconstruction, semi-convergence typically appears as gradual noise amplification during the later iterations.

# Solutions with Supplementary Information

## Prior knowledge on Fourier analysis

### Fourier convolution theorem

<div class="callout-tip" markdown="1">

#### Fourier convolution theorem

The Fourier convolution theorem states that convolution in one domain (e.g., image space) is equivalent to multiplication in the other domain (e.g., k-space). This is a fundamental principle in signal processing and MRI reconstruction. 

Specifically, if we have two functions $G(k)$ and $H(k)$ in k-space, their convolution in image space can be expressed as:

$$g(x) * h(x) \leftrightarrow G(k) \cdot H(k)$$

and conversely, if we have two functions $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ in image space, their convolution in k-space can be expressed as:

$$f(x) \cdot g(x) \leftrightarrow F(k) * G(k)$$

where $*$ denotes convolution and $\cdot$ denotes point-wise multiplication.

</div>

### Uncertainty principle or Fourier trade-off

<div class="callout-tip" markdown="1">

#### Fourier scaling theorem

The Fourier scaling theorem states that if a function $f(x)$ is scaled in the spatial domain by a factor of $a$, then its Fourier transform $F(k)$ is scaled in the frequency domain by a factor of $1/a$. Mathematically, this can be expressed as:

if 

$$\mathcal{F}\{f(x)\} = F(k)$$

then

$$\mathcal{F}\{f(ax)\} = \frac{1}{|a|} F\left(\frac{k}{a}\right)$$

In other words, if we compress a function in the spatial domain (i.e., make it narrower), its Fourier transform will be stretched in the frequency domain (i.e., become wider), and vice versa. This is a manifestation of the uncertainty principle in Fourier analysis, which states that there is a trade-off between the precision of a function in the spatial domain and its precision in the frequency domain.

</div>

## The "Halo" Artifact

Radial k-space trajectories are non-uniform, where the center part (low frequencies) of the k-space is over-sampled (denser) compared to the peripheral part (high frequencies). This non-uniformity has to be compensated for before gridding reconstruction. When k-space density is not corrected, the low-frequency signal dominates the reconstructed image, resulting in the "halo" artifact.

The density correction step in radial MRI reconstruction is analogous to the filtering step in filtered back projection reconstruction in CT.

How does density correction affect noise in the image?

Since the density correction is implemented as multiplying the k-space data with a ramp filter (i.e., the values are increasing radially), the noise in the k-space is no longer uniform. The low-frequency noise is dampened while the high-frequency noise is amplified. As a result, the high-frequency structure (edges, fine structures) is noisier.

## The "Freaking" Artifact

The streaking artifact is a signature of the radial k-space trajectory, when it was undersampled (i.e., not covering every point in k-space). To understand why it is "freaking" like that, let's think Fouirierly!

Undersampled k-space is equivalent to the multiplication of the fully-sampled k-space data by a masking function. The mask is simply an image with values of ones where the data were sampled and zeros where the data were not.

From the Fourier convolution theorem, multiplication in k-space means convolution in image space. Hence, the reconstructed image will be the convolution of the true image with the Point-Spread Function (i.e., a fancy name for the iFFT of the mask). As a result, the reconstructed image will exhibit the characteristic streaking artifacts due to the convolution with the PSF. ## How to Mitigate the "Freaking" Artifact?

NUFFT, strictly speaking, is a general form of gridding and inverse gridding. NUFFT alone cannot resolve the streaking artifacts in undersampled data, but it is used in every iteration of iterative reconstruction algorithms.

SENSE and GRAPPA are an image-based  and a k-space based parallel imaging techniques for reconstructing undersampled Cartesian k-space data. Out of the box, they cannot be directly applied to non-Cartesian data. 

CG-SENSE is an iterative reconstruction technique that can be used to reconstruct undersampled k-space data with an arbitrary k-space trajectory, including radial trajectories. CG-SENSE uses the Conjugate Gradient algorithm to solve the SENSE reconstruction problem iteratively, where the forward and adjoint operations are implemented using NUFFT for non-Cartesian trajectories.

Similar to the CT reconstruction problem, simple filtered back-projection reconstruction cannot resolve streaking artifacts when the data are collected with a reduced number of projections to reduce dose. However, a model-based iterative reconstruction can mitigate the streaking artifacts effectively, making reconstructed images diagnosable.
